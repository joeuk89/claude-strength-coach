## Answer

Take a light week when fatigue signals stack up, and at the latest when a
block reaches its ceiling of completed hard weeks (6 by default, 4 to 8 by
choice). A deload cuts sets and effort and usually load, and keeps the
exercises and the training days. The cadence and the counting rules are
coaching heuristics fitted to what experts and athletes report, not
thresholds any trial has tested.

## Evidence

**Claim 1.** The Delphi panel in bell-2023 agreed (100 %) that deloading
"could be pre-planned", "could be autoregulatory" and could be both. It
also agreed that deload frequency may depend on how the athlete responds,
and that a deload could occur "when athletes feel physically and mentally
fatigued regardless of training week". The panel does not rank
fatigue-triggered deloads above calendar ones; this framework chooses the
fatigue trigger and keeps a ceiling as the pre-planned backstop. coleman-2024
adds a reason not to take a week off by habit: resistance-trained adults
who stopped lifting for one week midway through a 9-week program gained
less lower-body strength than those who trained on, and no more muscle.

**Claims 2 and 11.** No study defines a "hard week" or a session that
counts toward one. The 75 % and 50 % lines, and the two-thirds and
1.5 RPE tests, are coaching rules chosen so a block counts training that
happened, not weeks that passed. The gate measures fatigue, not
compliance: a session with failed reps at RPE 10 passes it, and one with
no RPE logged counts as on target without its effort being measured, so
`block close` reports missed reps and effort well over or under target
beside the count rather than reading the gate as a completed prescription.

**Claim 3.** bell-2023 describes deloading as "generally undertaken every
4–6 weeks" and regular pre-planned deloading as "every 4–8 weeks".
rogerson-2024 surveyed 246 strength and physique athletes, who deloaded
every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks (range 1 to 12) for about 6 days. A default of 6 and
a range of 4 to 8 match that practice. Neither figure is one of the Delphi
panel's consensus statements: bell-2023 cites them from the literature,
and the panel set no interval, agreeing instead that deload frequency may
depend on how the athlete responds. Both sources count calendar or
training weeks, not completed hard weeks. Counting hard weeks is this
framework's adaptation, so the ceiling is a coaching heuristic. It can
space light weeks further apart than 8 calendar weeks.

**Claim 4.** No source sets a floor. The rule stops two bad weeks early in
a block from cutting it short before any hard training has built up.

**Claim 5.** bell-2023 supports deloading when fatigue builds, but no
study tests these four triggers or a two-trigger rule. The triggers are
the signals the coach can see each week (the athlete's reply, the log,
the niggle record and sleep).

**Claims 6 and 7.** The adherence lines are coaching heuristics, not
research cut-offs. They protect the program from adding work the athlete
is not already doing. Which weeks count is an application decision the
maintainer approved, not evidence: a week that lost planned training to
illness is left out of the window, while a reshuffle that only re-dates
the program's sessions stays in it with its misses counted.

**Claim 8.** bell-2023 reached universal agreement that volume should
fall during a deload, by fewer sets or fewer reps, and agreed a deload
"could be a reduction in proximity to failure". It found that intensity
could go up or down. rogerson-2024 reports what athletes do: fewer sets
and reps, lower loads on multi-joint (83.7 %) and single-joint (60.2 %)
lifts, effort reduced by leaving more reps in reserve, the same exercises
and the same training frequency.

**Claims 9 and 10.** The exact factors (two thirds, 10 %, RPE 6, RPE 8)
are this framework's defaults. They sit inside the reductions athletes in
rogerson-2024 report, but no study compares them with other sizes.
rogerson-2024 reports varied load reductions, not a rounding rule, so
the 10 % is a target: each line takes the nearest load the kit makes no
heavier than its working load, and the proposal reports the actual cut.
The RPE 6 and RPE 8 are ceilings, not targets: a 10 % cut usually leaves
the set easier than RPE 6, so the feasibility check (rn:autoregulation.6)
flags such a set only when it is too hard for its ceiling.

**Claim 12.** No source sets how often a lifter's training should be
reviewed in depth. 42 days is six calendar weeks, the same number as
claim 3's default ceiling, so a block that runs to its ceiling with no
missed weeks gets one deep review. A block that counts fewer hard weeks
than calendar weeks still gets one every six weeks. A light week proposed
again, unchanged and without a break, after the athlete declined it does
not make another check-in deep on its own: an application decision the maintainer
approved, which no source or claim sets.

**Claim 13.** This is bookkeeping, not a research finding. Claim 2's
50 % reset is for a normal program week that went badly; a week the
athlete and coach planned as different, or agreed to take off, is not
evidence about the block either way. Pausing the count keeps one agreed
break from ending one block per week it lasts, and the same break counts
the same whether or not an overlay was written for it. The days off still
count in full for the return bands (rn:detraining-return.3).

## Applying it

- `/check-in` keeps the hard-week count, checks the four triggers and
  proposes a light week by claims 3 to 5, counting a session toward a
  hard week by claim 11. The definitions live once in
  `CLAUDE.md` "Coaching principles" → Fatigue management.
- `/program-week`'s light mode builds a deload from the program's deload
  policy, or from claim 9 when the program has none.
- Claim 10 is the lighter option when the triggers are readiness and sleep
  rather than RPE drift or a niggle: the loads stay, so strength is kept,
  and the reduced sets and RPE ceiling let recovery catch up.
- `/check-in` schedules a deep review by claim 12.
- `block close` pauses the count for the weeks claim 13 names; a planned
  break is a status day added with `--no-training`.
- Treat claims 2 to 7 and 9 to 13 as rules of thumb. If the athlete's history
  shows they do not fit, write a local note that supersedes them.

## Open questions

- Does a fatigue-triggered deload beat a fixed calendar deload for
  strength or size? No trial compares them.
- Is a light week better than a full week off, or than no break at all,
  for trained lifters? coleman-2024 tested only a week off.
- How should cardio and coach-led sessions weigh in the count beyond
  "trained or not"?
