## Answer

Take a light week when fatigue signals stack up, and at the latest when a
block reaches its ceiling of completed hard weeks (6 by default, 4 to 8 by
choice). A deload cuts sets and effort and usually load, and keeps the
exercises and the training days. The cadence and the counting rules are
coaching heuristics fitted to what experts and athletes report, not
thresholds any trial has tested.

## Evidence

**Claim 1.** The Delphi panel in bell-2023 agreed (100 %) that deloading
"could be pre-planned", "could be autoregulatory" and could be both. It
also agreed that deload frequency may depend on how the athlete responds,
and that a deload could occur "when athletes feel physically and mentally
fatigued regardless of training week". The panel does not rank
fatigue-triggered deloads above calendar ones; this framework chooses the
fatigue trigger and keeps a ceiling as the pre-planned backstop. coleman-2024
adds a reason not to take a week off by habit: resistance-trained adults
who stopped lifting for one week midway through a 9-week program gained
less lower-body strength than those who trained on, and no more muscle.

**Claim 2.** No study defines a "hard week". The 75 % and 50 % lines are
coaching rules chosen so a block counts training that happened, not weeks
that passed.

**Claim 3.** bell-2023 describes deloading as "generally undertaken every
4–6 weeks" and regular pre-planned deloading as "every 4–8 weeks".
rogerson-2024 surveyed 246 strength and physique athletes, who deloaded
every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks (range 1 to 12) for about 6 days. A default of 6 and
a range of 4 to 8 match that practice. Both sources count calendar or
training weeks, not completed hard weeks, so counting hard weeks can space
light weeks further apart than 8 calendar weeks.

**Claim 4.** No source sets a floor. The rule stops two bad weeks early in
a block from cutting it short before any hard training has built up.

**Claim 5.** bell-2023 supports deloading when fatigue builds, but no
study tests these four triggers or a two-trigger rule. The triggers are
the signals the coach can see each week (the athlete's reply, the log,
the niggle record and sleep).

**Claims 6 and 7.** The adherence lines are coaching heuristics, not
research cut-offs. They protect the program from adding work the athlete
is not already doing.

**Claim 8.** bell-2023 reached universal agreement that volume should
fall during a deload, by fewer sets or fewer reps, and agreed a deload
"could be a reduction in proximity to failure". It found that intensity
could go up or down. rogerson-2024 reports what athletes do: fewer sets
and reps, lower loads on multi-joint (83.7 %) and single-joint (60.2 %)
lifts, effort reduced by leaving more reps in reserve, the same exercises
and the same training frequency.

**Claims 9 and 10.** The exact factors (two thirds, 10 %, RPE 6, RPE 8)
are this framework's defaults. They sit inside the reductions athletes in
rogerson-2024 report, but no study compares them with other sizes.

## Applying it

- `/check-in` keeps the hard-week count, checks the four triggers and
  proposes a light week by claims 3 to 5. The definitions live once in
  `CLAUDE.md` "Coaching principles" → Fatigue management.
- `/one-off-week` builds a deload from the program's deload policy, or
  from claim 9 when the program has none.
- Claim 10 is the lighter option when the triggers are readiness and sleep
  rather than RPE drift or a niggle: the loads stay, so strength is kept,
  and the reduced sets and RPE ceiling let recovery catch up.
- Treat claims 2 and 4 to 7 as rules of thumb. If the athlete's history
  shows they do not fit, write a local note that supersedes them.

## Open questions

- Does a fatigue-triggered deload beat a fixed calendar deload for
  strength or size? No trial compares them.
- Is a light week better than a full week off, or than no break at all,
  for trained lifters? coleman-2024 tested only a week off.
- How should cardio and coach-led sessions weigh in the count beyond
  "trained or not"?
