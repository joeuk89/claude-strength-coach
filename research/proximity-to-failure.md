## Answer

Take hypertrophy sets to about 0–3 reps in reserve, anywhere from about 5
to 30 reps. Build strength with about 1–6 reps at 80 % of 1RM or more,
leaving more reps in reserve than hypertrophy work does. Failure itself is
optional for both.

## Evidence

**Claim 1.** Robinson et al. (2024) estimated reps in reserve for every
set in the proximity-to-failure literature and found that "muscle
hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure". Refalo et
al. (2023, 15 studies) found "no evidence" that momentary failure beats
non-failure training for hypertrophy, and only a trivial advantage when
any definition of failure was used. Refalo et al. (2024) trained one leg
of resistance-trained lifters to failure and the other to 1–2 reps in
reserve for 8 weeks: quadriceps growth was similar. Grgic et al. (2022)
found no significant difference between failure and non-failure training
for hypertrophy overall, with a small advantage for failure in trained
lifters. Together: closer is better up to the last couple of reps, and
the last rep adds little. The 0–3 band captures that.

**Claim 2.** In Robinson et al., the slopes for strength "contained a null
point estimate": "strength gains were similar across a wide range of
RIR". Grgic et al. (2022) found no strength difference between failure and
non-failure training, and non-failure training won when sets were not
equated. The 2026 ACSM position stand lists training to momentary muscle
fatigue among the variables that "did not consistently impact training
outcomes". None of
these sets a reps-in-reserve number for strength work, so the framework
states the direction (more in reserve) rather than a band.

**Claim 3.** Currier et al. (2023, 178 strength studies) found that
"higher-load (>80% of single repetition maximum) prescriptions maximised
strength gains". The 2026 ACSM position stand lists heavier loads
"(≥80% one-repetition maximum)" first among the variables that enhance
strength. Lopez et al. (2021) and Schoenfeld et al. (2017) both found
strength gains greater with heavy loads than light ones. The 2009 ACSM
position stand recommends "eventual emphasis on heavy loading (1-6 RM)"
for intermediate and advanced lifters. The 1–6 rep figure is the rep
count that 80 % or more of 1RM allows with a few reps in reserve.

**Claim 4.** Schoenfeld et al. (2017, 21 studies, all to failure) found
that hypertrophy "can be equally achieved across a spectrum of loading
ranges", comparing loads at or under 60 % of 1RM with heavier ones.
Lopez et al. (2021, 28 studies, all to failure) found no hypertrophy
difference between low (more than 15 RM), moderate (9–15 RM) and high
(8 RM or fewer) loads. Currier et al. (2023) found "all prescriptions
comparably promoted muscle hypertrophy". The 5–30 band is the practical
span of those studies; very light loads (above about 30 reps) are less
well tested and much more tiring per set.

## Applying it

- Prescribe hypertrophy work at RPE 7–10 (0–3 reps in reserve), closer
  to failure on isolation and machine work, further on hard compound
  lifts where form breaks first.
- Prescribe heavy strength work at 1–6 reps, 80 % of 1RM or more, with
  more reps in reserve than hypertrophy work so technique holds.
- Pick rep ranges for joints, equipment and preference: at equal effort,
  8 and 25 reps grow muscle about equally.

## Open questions

- Whether reps-in-reserve targets near failure produce the same growth
  in lifters who misjudge reps in reserve badly.
- Whether a reps-in-reserve band for heavy strength work can be stated
  from direct trials rather than inferred.
