## Answer

Beginners can add load every session, intermediates week to week or over
a short wave, and advanced lifters over blocks of weeks. Match the
progression scheme to that pace: linear, then double progression or a
wave, then RPE-anchored. Any scheme works as long as it keeps adding
stimulus.

## Evidence

**Claim 1.** Rhea et al. (2003, 140 studies) found that effect sizes
"demonstrated different responses based on the training status of the
participants": untrained lifters gained most at about 60 % of 1RM and
trained lifters needed about 80 %. The 2009 ACSM position stand moves
lifters from 8–12 RM loads as novices to "a wider loading range from 1
to 12 RM" with "eventual emphasis on heavy loading (1-6 RM)" as they
advance.

**Claim 2.** The 2009 ACSM position stand recommends "that 2-10% increase
in load be applied when the individual can perform the current workload
for one to two repetitions over the desired number". Double progression
is that rule in scheme form: reps rise within a range, and reaching the
top at or under the target RPE triggers the smallest available load step.
The ACSM figure is a consensus recommendation, not a trial result.

**Claims 3–5.** No trial compares how fast load can rise at each training
age. The 2009 ACSM position stand defines intermediate lifters as having
"approximately 6 months of consistent RT experience" and advanced as
"years of RT experience", and recommends periodized programs beyond the
novice stage. The pace (session, week, block) is the long-standing
coaching model, consistent with claim 1's slowing response but not
measured directly. These claims are heuristics, and they map onto the
framework's starter programs: `linear` for beginners, double progression
or the 3-week wave for intermediates, and the RPE-anchored scheme for
intermediate to advanced lifters.

**Claim 6.** Hickmott et al. (2022, 15 studies) found that autoregulated
and standardized load prescription "produced similar improvements in
strength". Zhang et al. (2021, 8 studies in athletes) found the
auto-regulation method beat fixed loading for maximum strength (effect
size 0.64). The two disagree on size, not direction: autoregulation is
at least as good.

**Claim 7.** The 2026 ACSM position stand, which updates the 2009 one,
lists periodization among the variables that "did not consistently
impact training outcomes", while still recommending that "healthy adults should perform
progressive RT". The scheme matters less than the athlete actually
progressing on it.

## Applying it

- Read the athlete's experience from the profile, then check it against
  the log: a lifter who still adds load every session is a beginner for
  that lift, whatever their years.
- Move a lift from `linear` to double progression once session-to-session
  jumps start to fail, not on a calendar date.
- Use RPE-anchored top sets when daily readiness swings a lot (sleep,
  work, cardio load) or progress has slowed to block-to-block.

## Open questions

- No direct data on how many weeks each training-age stage lasts per lift.
- Whether autoregulation's advantage in athletes holds for recreational
  lifters who misjudge RPE.
