## Answer

Coach protein toward 1.6 g/kg/day of a reference mass, with 2.2 g/kg/day
as the most that still helps and a reachable floor no lower than
1.2 g/kg/day. The reference mass is fat-free mass with a credible
body-fat reading, goal weight when body fat is high, and bodyweight
otherwise. Cut at ~0.5–1 % of bodyweight per week and bulk at
~0.25–0.5 lb per week.

## Evidence

**Claims 1 and 2.** Morton et al. (2018, 49 trials, 1,863 participants)
found that "protein supplementation beyond total protein intakes of
1.62 g/kg/day resulted in no further RET-induced gains in FFM". The full
text gives the 95 % confidence interval as 1.03 to 2.20 g/kg/day and
says "it may be prudent to recommend ~2.2 g protein/kg/d for those
seeking to maximise resistance training-induced gains in FFM". Nunes et
al. (2022, 74 trials) found the lean-mass benefit significant in lifters
under 65 eating 1.6 g/kg/day or more. Tagawa et al. (2021, 105 articles)
found lean mass still rose above 1.3 g/kg/day, but by about a third as
much per 0.1 g/kg/day as below it. The ISSN position stand (Jäger et al.
2017) puts 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day as "sufficient for most exercising
individuals". The same stand says "higher protein intakes (2.3-3.1
g/kg/d) may be needed to maximize the retention of lean body mass in
resistance-trained subjects during hypocaloric periods", so it backs
claim 1 but not claim 2's ceiling. This note handles a deficit through
the fat-free-mass figures in claim 6, not by raising the bodyweight
stretch. All of these express protein per kg of total bodyweight in
mostly lean or normal-weight people.

**Claim 3.** No trial compares reference masses directly, so this is a
coaching rule. It follows from how the evidence is reported: Morton and
Nunes per kg of bodyweight in mostly non-obese people, Helms et al.
(2014) per kg of fat-free mass in lean lifters, and Weijs and Wolfe (2016)
per kg of both in adults with obesity. Per kg of bodyweight, an athlete
carrying a lot of fat would be set a number well above what their muscle
needs. Goal weight or fat-free mass keeps the number reachable.

**Claim 4.** ACE's percent body fat norms list "Obesity" as above 25 %
for men and above 32 % for women. Research more often uses the WHO lines
of above 25 % for men and above 35 % for women (Romero-Corral et al.
2008), so the women's line here is the stricter of the two (see Open
questions). These lines are applied to rougher readings (a smart scale or
a tape method), where the error is a few percentage points either way.

**Claim 5.** Romero-Corral et al. (2008, 13,601 adults) found BMI 30 or
more had high specificity but "poor sensitivity" (36 % in men, 49 % in
women) for excess body fat: it "misses more than half of people with
excess fat". The goal-weight test catches the rest. The 10 % figure is a
coaching rule.

**Claim 6.** The conversion is arithmetic: 1.6 g/kg at 15–20 % body fat
is 1.9–2.0 g/kg of fat-free mass, and 2.2 g/kg is 2.6–2.75 g/kg.
Helms et al. (2014, 6 studies) concluded that "protein needs for
energy-restricted resistance-trained athletes are likely 2.3-3.1g/kg of
FFM scaled upwards with severity of caloric restriction and leanness".
Helms, Aragon and Fitschen (2014) give the same range for contest
preparation. Morton sets the target and stretch it converts.

**Claim 7.** Kokura et al. (2024, 47 trials in adults with overweight or
obesity aiming to lose weight) found that "an intake exceeding 1.3
g/kg/day is anticipated to increase muscle mass, while an intake below
1.0 g/kg/day is associated with a higher risk of muscle mass decline".
Weijs and Wolfe (2016, 60 older adults with obesity on a 13-week diet and
resistance training) found muscle gain far more likely above 1.2 g/kg of
bodyweight or 1.9 g/kg of fat-free mass (72 % versus 33 % of subjects).
Both studied populations heavier and often older than the typical
athlete here, so the claim carries low confidence. The fat-free-mass
figure is 1.2 g/kg converted at ~20 % body fat.

**Claim 8.** A coaching rule with no direct trial. It sets a floor the
athlete can meet now and leaves the target as the direction of travel.

**Claim 9.** A coaching rule for building the habit. Tagawa et al.
(2021) found each extra 0.1 g/kg/day of protein added lean mass across
0.5 to 3.5 g/kg/day, so small steps up are worth taking. For a 75–100 kg
reference mass, 0.1–0.15 g/kg/day is about 10–15 g.

**Claim 10.** Murphy and Koehler (2022) found lean-mass gains "impaired"
in resistance training with an energy deficit compared with without one
(effect size -0.57), while strength gains were "comparable". Their
meta-regression "demonstrated that an energy deficit of ~500 kcal · day-1
prevented gains in LM", and they advise that people preserving lean mass
during weight loss "should avoid energy deficits >500 kcal day-1".

**Claim 11.** Garthe et al. (2011) randomised 24 elite athletes to lose
0.7 % or 1.4 % of bodyweight a week (they achieved 0.7 % and 1.0 %).
Lean body mass rose 2.1 % in the slower group and did not change in the
faster one, and the authors advise aiming for 0.7 % a week. Helms, Aragon
and Fitschen (2014) recommend losses of "approximately 0.5 to 1%/wk to
maximize muscle retention". Murphy and Koehler's 500 kcal/day threshold
points the same way: a smaller deficit keeps more lean mass. The
"slower near the goal" part is a coaching rule: leaner athletes need a
smaller deficit (Helms et al. 2014).

**Claim 12.** Helms et al. (2023) randomised 21 trained lifters to
maintenance, a 5 % surplus or a 15 % surplus for 8 weeks. Their
regression found "faster rates of BM gain (and by proxy larger surpluses)
primarily increase rates of fat gain rather than augmenting 1-RM or MT".
Iraki et al. (2019) recommend "a target weight gain of ~0.25-0.5% of
bodyweight/week for novice/intermediate bodybuilders", and slower for
advanced ones. For an 80 kg lifter that is about 0.45–0.9 lb a week, so
the framework's 0.25–0.5 lb is about half as fast. That makes it a
heuristic: the sources support the direction (slow), not the figure
(see Open questions).

**Claim 13.** MacroFactor's help centre names "partial food logging" as
its algorithms' "one major Achilles heel" and advises estimating a missed
day instead: an estimate "within about 30% of what you actually consumed"
is close enough. This is the app maker's guidance, not a trial.

## Applying it

- `/goals` picks the reference mass (claims 3–5), then records the floor,
  target and stretch from it (claims 1, 2, 6–8). Round each to the
  nearest 5 g.
- `/check-in` raises the floor by ~10–15 g after two consecutive check-ins
  at or above it (claim 9) and never lowers it.
- On a cut, hold the trend to ~0.5–1 % of bodyweight a week and push
  protein toward the stretch as the athlete gets leaner (claims 6, 10,
  11). On a bulk, hold the trend to ~0.25–0.5 lb a week (claim 12).
- App users log every day; a missed day gets an estimate, not a gap
  (claim 13).

## Open questions

- Whether the women's high-body-fat line should be ~35 % (WHO and most
  research) rather than ACE's ~32 %.
- Whether the bulk rate should be stated as a share of bodyweight
  (~0.25–0.5 % a week, as Iraki et al. recommend) rather than in pounds.
- How well the 1.2 g/kg floor, drawn from adults with obesity, holds for
  lean, younger lifters.
