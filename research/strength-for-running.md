## Answer

Strength work may support running, but economy and performance are distinct outcomes.
Use the evidence to discuss the athlete's priorities and tolerance, not to mandate more lifting.

## Evidence

**Claim 1.** Economy is the oxygen or energy cost at a given submaximal
speed. The economy review pooled controlled studies in 652 runners aged
17–45, mostly male, from moderately to highly trained. Heavy lifting,
jumps and combinations were compared with running plus no or low-load
strength work. Certainty was moderate for single methods and low for
combined methods; submaximal-load and isometric groups had no clear pooled
benefit. A laboratory economy change does not establish a faster race.

**Claim 2.** The performance review pooled 894 runners aged 17–40, mostly
male. It measured time trials and time to exhaustion, with very low to
moderate certainty across outcomes. Plyometrics alone had no clear pooled
performance benefit. These outcomes do not establish identical effects
across race distances, sexes, training histories or older recreational
runners; the claim carries low confidence for coaching transfer.

**Claims 1–2, current-source check (2026-10-11).** The 2025 umbrella review
supports the broad direction but rates most underlying reviews as low or
critically low methodological confidence. Overlapping reviews are not
independent replications and do not justify upgrading this note to high
confidence. Publisher records and later-work searches were checked for
updates and corrections to the two 2024 papers.

**Claim 3** is a coaching scope rule, not an experimentally established
program. None of these comparisons proves that an already strength-trained
runner needs additional sets, jumps or a specific weekly schedule.

## Applying it

- Consult when an outside running plan or event makes lifting's contribution
  relevant. Explain whether the cited outcome is economy or performance.
- Review current lifting, running priorities, experience, fatigue and
  limitations with the athlete; keep routine changes in the usual approval
  flow. Apply existing interference checks alongside this evidence.
- Keep running in the review tier: the athlete or outside plan owns its
  progression. This note neither creates planned activities nor activates
  coach-planned running; that program-tier work remains deferred.

## Open questions

- Which lifters who already run benefit from changing their current program?
- How well do laboratory outcomes transfer to the athlete's event and terrain?
- Which methods suit older runners or those with injury limitations?
