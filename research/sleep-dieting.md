## Answer

Adults need 7 or more hours of sleep a night. Short sleep during a
deficit makes more of the weight lost come from fat-free mass, so a
trailing sleep average under ~7 h means recomp at maintenance instead of
a cut. The evidence for the body-composition effect rests on two small
trials, so confidence is low.

## Evidence

**Claim 1.** The AASM and Sleep Research Society joint consensus (Watson
et al. 2015) states: "Adults should sleep 7 or more hours per night on a
regular basis to promote optimal health." It adds that "sleeping less
than 7 hours per night on a regular basis is associated with adverse
health outcomes, including weight gain and obesity".

**Claim 2.** Nedeltcheva et al. (2010) ran a randomised crossover trial
in 10 adults with overweight: 14 days of moderate caloric restriction
with 8.5 or 5.5 hours of sleep opportunity. "Sleep curtailment decreased
the proportion of weight lost as fat by 55% (1.4 vs. 0.6 kg)" and
"increased the loss of fat-free body mass by 60% (1.5 vs. 2.4 kg)".
Wang et al. (2018) randomised 36 adults with overweight or obesity to
8 weeks of caloric restriction alone or with about 1 hour less sleep on
five nights a week. Both groups lost similar weight, but the share of it
lost as fat was significantly greater with normal sleep, and "catch-up"
sleep on the other two nights did not undo the effect. Neither trial
included resistance training, and both were small and short.

**Claim 3.** The ~7 h line is Watson et al.'s adult minimum; the trials
behind claim 2 show what is at stake when sleep falls short during a
deficit. Recomp at maintenance avoids that trade. The 14-day window,
which smooths night-to-night noise in tracker data, is a coaching rule.
No trial tests a sleep threshold for starting a cut.

**Claim 4.** A coaching rule: without a measurement, the cautious
default protects lean mass until a sleep source confirms the athlete
sleeps enough.

**Claim 5.** Tasali et al. (2022) randomised 80 adults with overweight
who slept under 6.5 hours to sleep-hygiene counselling or their usual
sleep. Sleep rose by about 1.2 hours a night, and energy intake fell by
270 kcal/day against the control group, with weight lost. Fixing sleep
makes a later deficit easier to hold, which is why the gate sends the
athlete to recomp rather than to a harder diet.

## Applying it

- `/goals` checks the sleep gate before any deficit (claims 3 and 4) and
  records the result.
- `/check-in` re-checks the gate during a deficit once a sleep source
  exists.
- When sleep is the limiter, coach sleep first (claim 5); the deficit
  can start once the trailing average reaches ~7 h.

## Open questions

- Whether the effect in claim 2 holds for lifters who train hard and eat
  enough protein during the deficit: neither trial tested it.
- Whether sleep measured by a consumer watch or phone, which can misjudge
  sleep by tens of minutes, is accurate enough for a ~7 h line.
