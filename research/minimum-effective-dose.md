## Answer

A reduced dose can maintain adaptations when intensity stays high, but
retention depends on age and the preceding training dose. The app compares
minimum weeks with ordinary programmed weeks as a coaching policy, not a
universal guarantee; older lifters may need substantially more work.

## Evidence

**Claim 1.** Bickel, Cross and Bamman (2011) trained 70 adults 3 days a
week for 16 weeks, then randomised them for 32 weeks to stop or to
maintain at one third or one ninth of the dose. In the young (20–35
years), "both maintenance prescriptions preserved phase 1 muscle
hypertrophy", and the one-third dose even added some myofiber size.
Strength was largely kept in every group, even after stopping. Spiering
et al. (2021), a narrative review, concludes that strength and size can
be kept "for up to 32 weeks with as little as 1 session of strength
training per week and 1 set per exercise, as long as exercise intensity"
is maintained, and that intensity "seems to be the key variable". One
third describes a reduction from prior training, not a fraction of a generic
hypertrophy target; it is not a guarantee for every lifter.

**Claim 2.** In Bickel et al., neither maintenance dose kept the
hypertrophy of the 60–75-year-olds, who "require a higher dose of weekly
loading than the young". Spiering et al. suggest older adults may need
up to 2 sessions a week and 2–3 sets per exercise to keep size.

**Claim 3.** Androulakis-Korakakis, Fisher and Steele (2020, 6 studies)
found that "a single set performed minimum 1 time and maximum 3 times per
week was sufficient to induce significant 1RM strength gains", about
12 kg on average across the lifts, and recommend "a single set of 6-12
repetitions with loads ranging from approximately 70-85% 1RM 2-3 times
per week". The studies are few and in trained men only, hence low
confidence on the claim.

**Claim 4.** The floor of 2 hard sets is a coaching heuristic: one set
builds strength (claim 3), and a second gives a margin for a bad day
without adding much time. It is not a tested figure.

**Claim 5.** The 2 working sets a folded exercise carries by default
rest on the same reasoning as claim 4. Spiering et al. and
Androulakis-Korakakis et al. support 1 set per exercise as the floor;
the second set is a margin, chosen so the session it lands in stays
short. It is not a tested figure.

**Claim 6.** The app applies an approximate one-third comparison to each
muscle's fractional hard sets in the corresponding ordinary program week.
Every coach-led estimate the ordinary week counts enters the reference; only
sessions explicitly retained in the minimum week enter its planned dose. Bickel studied reductions
from prior exercise dose, not this fractional-muscle calculation. The check
is application policy and passing it does not prove maintenance, especially
for older lifters.

## Applying it

- In a disrupted stretch, cut sets before load: keep each lift at its
  prescribed load and RPE. Compare reduced sets with the ordinary program's
  dose, keeping the age and evidence limits above visible.
- For an athlete in their 60s or older, keep maintenance nearer normal
  training: about 2 sessions a week and 2–3 sets per exercise.
- When time is very short, one hard set per main lift, 2–3 times a week,
  still moves strength forward.
- When a missed session's exercise moves into a later session, give it 2
  working sets unless the program's rule says otherwise.

## Open questions

- How long maintenance at one third holds in trained lifters with years
  of training, rather than after a 16-week program.
- Whether the minimum dose for 1RM strength also holds for women and for
  lifts other than the squat and bench press.
