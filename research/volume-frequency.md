## Answer

Give each muscle about 10–20 hard sets a week, counting indirect sets as
half. Spread them over about 2 sessions per muscle, keeping each session
to about 11 fractional sets for that muscle. At equal weekly sets, more
frequent training is a scheduling choice for size but adds a little
strength.

## Evidence

**Claim 1.** Pelland et al. (2026, 67 studies, 2,058 participants) found
a 100 % posterior probability that both hypertrophy and strength rise
with weekly sets, with diminishing returns, sharper for strength.
Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2017) found each extra weekly set added
about 0.37 % to the gain in muscle size, with a trend favouring 10 or
more sets a week over fewer (P = 0.074). The 2026 ACSM position stand (an overview of 137 reviews)
lists "higher volumes (≥10 sets/wk)" as one of the few variables that
enhance hypertrophy. The lower bound of 10 is well supported. The upper
bound of 20 is not: Pelland et al. found no clear plateau for
hypertrophy, and their companion per-session paper (Remmert et al.)
reports the weekly point past which extra sets show no detectable
advantage at about 31 fractional sets. So the claim is a heuristic: 20
is a recoverable working ceiling, not where gains stop. Going above 20
is a deliberate, short-term choice for a lagging muscle, not a default.

**Claim 2.** Pelland et al. scored each indirect set as 1 ("total"), 0.5
("fractional") or 0 ("direct"). "The relative evidence for the
'fractional' quantification method was strongest", so the primary models
use it. A bench press set therefore counts 1 for chest and 0.5 for
triceps.

**Claim 3.** Remmert et al. (2025) ran the same kind of meta-regression on
per-session sets. The point of undetectable outcome superiority, the
per-session count past which no extra sets showed a meaningful advantage,
was "~11 'fractional' sets for hypertrophy" and about 2 direct sets for
strength. The authors stress this is not an upper limit beyond which
gains stop, and data at high per-session counts are sparse. The paper is
a SportRxiv preprint, not yet peer reviewed (checked 2026-10-11), so the
claim carries low confidence. Refresh it when a peer-reviewed version
appears.

**Claim 4.** Schoenfeld, Grgic and Krieger (2019, 25 studies) found "no
significant difference between higher and lower frequency on a
volume-equated basis" for hypertrophy and concluded that lifters "can
choose a weekly frequency per muscle groups based on personal
preference". For strength, Pelland et al. found a 100 % posterior
probability that strength rises with frequency after adjusting for
volume, with diminishing returns. Grgic et al. (2018) found frequency
helped strength overall but not significantly in volume-equated studies;
Pelland et al. reconcile the two, noting Grgic's effect size still rose
from 1 to 2 sessions a week. The strength benefit likely comes partly
from more practice of the lift.

**Claim 5.** Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2016) found that training a
muscle twice a week grew it more than once a week (effect size 0.49
versus 0.30). Currier et al. (2023, 119 hypertrophy studies) ranked
higher-load, multi-set, twice-weekly training highest for hypertrophy.
The 2026 ACSM position stand lists "≥2 sessions/wk" among the variables
that enhance strength. In Pelland et al. the largest strength step is
from 1 to 2 sessions a week, after which returns shrink. Twice a week is
also what claim 3 requires once weekly sets pass about 11 for a muscle.

## Applying it

- Size each muscle's weekly dose at 10–20 fractional sets, starting near
  10 for a beginner or a muscle that is only being maintained.
- When a session plans more than about 11 fractional sets for one muscle,
  move the extra sets to another session that week rather than dropping
  them.
- Choose frequency to fit the athlete's week: at equal weekly sets, 2
  sessions per muscle is the default, 3 suits a strength priority, and 1
  is acceptable for a muscle that only needs maintaining.
- Count sets in coach-led sessions too; weekly balance covers every
  session.

## Open questions

- Whether a peer-reviewed version of Remmert et al. confirms the
  per-session figure.
- How far above 20 weekly sets is still recoverable for a lifter who is
  also running or doing conditioning work.
