## Answer

Watch single runs, not ratios: a run more than 10 % longer than the
longest run of the previous 30 days was linked to more overuse injuries
in a large cohort, while acute:chronic ratios and week-to-week change were
not. Session-RPE load is a valid way to sum up a week's internal load, so
the coach reports it, but no flag acts on it. The history guard and the
quality-session defaults are coaching heuristics.

## Evidence

**Claim 1.** frandsen-2025 followed 5,205 runners for 18 months through
588,071 GPS-logged sessions; 1,311 reported an overuse injury. Against
runs at most 10 % past the longest run of the previous 30 days, the
adjusted hazard rate ratio for overuse injury was 1.64 (95 % CI 1.31 to
2.05) for runs 10–30 % longer, 1.52 (1.16 to 2.00) for 30–100 % longer and
2.28 (1.50 to 3.48) for more than double. It is one observational study,
with no trial behind it, so the claim carries the note's low confidence.
The 1.10 ratio and the 30-day window are the study's own exposure
definition.

**Claim 2.** In the same cohort, frandsen-2025 found no raised risk from
acute:chronic workload ratio spikes (0.75 to 0.94; only the large-spike
estimate, 0.75, was significant, and it pointed to lower risk) or from week-to-week change (0.88
to 0.97, none significant). impellizzeri-2020 argues that no study has
estimated a causal effect of the ratio on injury, so managing load by it
is conjecture; lolli-2019 shows that the usual calculation, with the
acute week inside the chronic window, builds in a spurious correlation.

**Claim 3.** foster-2001 proposed session RPE × duration on the CR-10
scale and found it tracked a heart-rate-based load across steady and
intermittent exercise. haddad-2017 reviewed 36 studies that tested the
method and found it valid, reliable and internally consistent across
sports, sexes, ages and levels. A valid measure of load is not a
predictor of injury, and no threshold in it has been tied to injury risk,
so the coach reports it and no flag reads it (claim 2).

**Claim 4.** A run's spike is judged against the longest run in the 30
days before it. With a short history that longest run is a poor
reference, so a run is judged only once the logs reach 14 days back.
The 14 days is a coaching judgement; no study tests it.

**Claims 5–7.** Coaching defaults with no study behind the numbers. Two
quality sessions a week (claim 5) is a common cap for a lifter who also
runs. Claim 6 puts the quality line at RPE 7, "very hard" on foster-2001's
CR-10 scale. Claim 7 fills a coach-led session the athlete logged no
detail for with a typical hour at a hard effort (RPE 6).

## Applying it

- Flag a run over 1.10 × the longest run of the previous 30 days; say
  that it is a risk marker, not a diagnosis.
- Report the week's session-RPE load beside the run numbers, for
  information. Never compute or act on a ratio of it.
- Skip the spike check, and say so, while the activity history is
  shorter than 14 days before the run.
- An athlete can change claims 5–7 in `[cardio]`; they are defaults.

## Open questions

- Does the spike link hold for runners who also lift, whose leg load
  comes from more than running?
- Should duration spikes count the same as distance spikes? frandsen-2025
  measured distance only.
- Would a session-RPE spike, rather than a distance spike, flag risk
  better for mixed training?
